What was here first

Houston schools were the oldest institutions in their neighborhoods, and generations of the same families moved through them. A third-grade math teacher brought in waffles to explain fractions — is one half bigger, or one fourth? The children who saw the waffle never forgot which.

“Before the takeover, we had an elected school board. There were issues, there were allegations of corruption, but if a school board member did something egregious, we could vote them out…”— MinhDan Tran · Teacher

What happened

In 2023 the Texas Education Agency removed Houston's elected school board and installed a Board of Managers it appointed. More than 180,000 students, most of them Black and Latine. The stated reason was the performance of a single high school — Wheatley, a historically Black school in a neighborhood the state had disinvested in for decades.

Parents did not vote on this. They were not asked.

“Before the takeover, the role of our elected school board was to operate as a liaison between community and the district. So it was legally required for them to communicate with us. After getting this board of managers and appointed superintendent, that all changed. They don't have to answer to us. They answer to TEA.”— Kourtney Revels · Parent / organizer

What stolen power looked like in Houston’s schools

The librarian was fired first. The library became a storage room, then a room they send children to when they fail the daily quiz. The paints went. The bathrooms got locked for the first twenty minutes of every class, and the last twenty. Every lesson got a timer — thirty seconds to discuss, three minutes a slide. The books are still in the library. The children can see them through the glass. They are not allowed to touch them.

Eleven of the twenty-one people we interviewed reached for the same word, unprompted, across eight different schools. None of them had met each other.

“After the state takeover, school for my child became like prison. And those are her words.”— Kourtney Revels · Parent / organizer
“After the school takeover, the school felt more like a prison or like a jail. They watch us 24 7 and they don't like to actually listen to what we have to say.”— Zachary Youngblood · Student
“Being a student HISD feels like being a prisoner.”— Drapon Allen · Student

Who decides now

Houston kept voting. It stopped mattering.

Houston kept voting after the takeover. The elections still happen. What changed is what a vote buys.

“We did have an election this past November where we elected four school board positions, but the people we elected have no voting power, so they're kind of there.”— MinhDan Tran · Teacher
“It was our way of saying, you know what? We still care about what happens in our school. We're still going to elect people that we know are going to do better, but at the same time, they can go to board meetings, but they have no vote. They have no power.”— MinhDan Tran · Teacher

What people want

Asked what they actually wanted, nobody reached for policy language. They named the thing itself.

“The single biggest change I would make to improve my experience at HISD right now is to end the takeover and give the power back to the community.”— Toshiba Dixon · Parent
“I don't see any positive in it. I think they need to give the power back to the communities.”— Toshiba Dixon · Parent

Nobody in eighteen interviews said home rule, self-determination, or local control. Asked what they wanted, they said give the power back.
