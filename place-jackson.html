What we built

Jackson built its own water system, and Jackson paid for it. For generations the people who lived here built the infrastructure, paid the taxes that maintained it, and elected the people who ran it.

Jackson also built something most American cities do not have: a People’s Assembly — an actual structure where residents make decisions together and the city governs alongside them rather than at them. That is not a metaphor for democracy. It is a calendar, a room, and a vote.

What happened

After the 2022 crisis left the city without safe drinking water, the state moved to regionalize the system — shifting control outward toward surrounding counties that are smaller, whiter, and not the people who built or use it.

In the same period the legislature passed HB 1020, expanding a state-appointed court system and a state-controlled police force inside the state’s Blackest city, with judges appointed rather than elected by the people they judge.

What it feels like

First you cannot drink it. Then you cannot decide about it.

The clearest description of what that does to a community came from Jackson itself — not as a legal argument, but as a plain statement of what was agreed and then undone.

“One state can change a law we agreed to as a community.”— Rukia Lumumba

Notice the possessives. We agreed. Our system. That is not rhetoric. It is a description of who paid for the pipes.

Who decides now

A city that governs by assembly, governed by appointment. Jackson has one of the most developed models of shared community governance in the country, and it is being run, in significant part, by people its residents did not choose.

Both things are true at once, and the second one is not an accident of history. It is the response to the first.
