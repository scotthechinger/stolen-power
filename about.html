About this project

How this site was made, and who it is for.

Why these four places

Houston, Jackson, Virginia and St. Louis are anchor places, not the whole story. Stolen power is moving through statehouses across the country — these are the four where this campaign has partners on the ground, documentation, and people who agreed to talk on the record.

They are not the worst four, or the only four. They are the four we can show you in depth. For everything else, the map and the tracker are the honest picture.

Where the words come from

Everything quoted on this site was said by a real person on the record. The Houston material comes from eighteen interviews with twenty-one people across eight schools, captured in May 2026. Nothing has been tidied, merged, or paraphrased.

Where a claim rests on a listening session rather than field capture, we say so on the page rather than letting it pass as documented.

What we mean by stolen power

“Preemption” is what lawyers and legislators call the long-standing, racist practice of stealing local power. It is a real word for a real legal tool. But legalese works like a wall — the more neutral or dull sounding the name, the easier it is to overlook, and the longer it survives.

So we call it what it is.

The question worth asking

Not “what would you do if you had power” — that is a hard question to answer cold, and it invites an argument about definitions.

The better one, and the one we started asking in listening sessions:

What does it feel like the day after you get it back?

Asked cold, the affirmative question underperformed badly. Asked after people had lived the loss, it produced immediate, concrete vision. Nobody said “restore local governance.” Everybody said something specific: reshape the police department, build a citizen review board, fix bail, raise revenue without raising taxes on the people who live here, restore the citizen ballot initiative, end the takeover and give the power back.

What we are actually asking for

Not a policy. A default.

That the people who live somewhere get to decide about it. That a city which builds a water system gets to run it. That parents who elect a school board get a school board. That when a community organizes for years and wins something, the win is allowed to survive contact with a statehouse four hours away.

That your caring is not treated as a threat, and your organizing is not treated as a warning.

It has been done before

St. Louis organized for a hundred and fifty years and won local control of its police by ballot initiative in 2012. Jackson built a People’s Assembly and governs through it. Virginia is drafting home rule right now, for the first time in the state’s history. Houston parents wrote one email about a door-locking policy endangering their children, and the policy changed the same day — then they did it again at a second school, and it worked again.

The fight has a long history of winning. That is why they keep having to do this.

They don’t attack what doesn’t matter.
