How it happens

You already know how this feels.
You may not know what it's called.

Three minutes. No legal vocabulary. We start with a problem you have probably had.

↓ Scroll

The problem

Something is wrong, and it is clear to you what’s needed.

It is not a mystery. It is not a policy debate. It's the kind of thing where everyone who lives near you already agrees, because everyone who lives near you can see it too.

It just needs fixing.

Pick one

Which problem do you know best?

Choose the one you can relate most to. We'll walk you through what you would do about it — and who is actually allowed to decide.

Your home
Your home

So you do the thing you're supposed to do.

You talk to your neighbors and find out it isn't just your unit. It's the building. Maybe it's the block.

Who do you take it to?

You tried that. Twice. That's how you ended up looking for someone with actual authority.
Right. The people you voted for, whose office is a bus ride away, whose names you know.
Not theirs to fix. Housing conditions should be a local decision, made locally.

So you go to your city council. You speak in the two minutes they give you. You bring people. You come back next month, and the month after.

And it works.

The council votes yes. Your city passes a tenant protection, or a heat standard, or a cap on what a landlord can raise in a year.

You won. Not because anyone did you a favor — because you organized and the people who should make decisions were close enough to reach.

So that's it. You won. Right?

That is what winning a vote is supposed to mean.
Somebody can. And they will — from a building far away you have never been inside.

Keep going.

Then, hours away, in another city you may have never visited —

What you did

Organized your neighbors. Went to every meeting. Made the argument. Won a vote in a body you elected, in the city where you pay rent and taxes.

“We have a voice in this house.”

What they did

Passed a sentence or two making your rule illegal. Not just yours — any rule like it, in any city in the state, forever.

Nobody came to tell you. You found out when nothing changed.

The problem was the same. The organizing was the same. You were the same. The only thing that changed was who was allowed to decide.

And now the part that isn't hypothetical

In Virginia, you wouldn't have gotten as far as the vote.

Virginia cities have never had what’s called “home rule.” Not lost it — never had it.

Virginia runs on something called Dillon's Rule: a locality can only do what the state has explicitly given it permission to do. Everything else requires asking. A Virginia city generally cannot raise its own minimum wage. It often cannot protect its tenants. It cannot decide how to raise its own revenue without going to Richmond first.

Everything. It means everything.

They can't even install a speed bump in front of a school without state permission.— Maria Robalino, Virginia Home Rule Project

Local officials describe it as begging for the leftovers of power.

The result is more than just stolen power.

Ask Virginia residents what they'd do with real local power and they answer immediately. They have lists. They've been waiting.

Ask local officials the same question and many of them can't picture it — because they have never once governed in a world where it existed.

That is what a hundred and fifty years of permission does. It doesn't only take your power. Eventually it takes your ability to imagine having had it.

A bill is being drafted right now to change that, for the first time in the state's history.

Your kid's school
Your child's school

So you do the thing you're supposed to do.

You email the teacher. You ask the principal. You find out from other parents in the group chat or pickup line that it isn't just your kid's class — it's the whole school, and probably the school down the road.

Who actually decides this?

The principal is following a policy somebody else wrote. Keep going up.
Right. In almost every district in America, you help elect it.
Hold that thought.

So you go to the school board — the piece of government closest to your life, and you get a vote in who sits on it. That is the entire point of a school board.

And that used to be enough.

Not perfect. But at least reachable.

What made it reachable?

Part of it. But one parent in Houston put it more concretely than that.
Yes. Accountability is not a complaints process. It is a person you can run into.
There is always a complaints process. That is not the same thing.
I knew who my board member was. I knew where they lived. They went to Phyllis Wheatley. I'm a community leader. I ran into her all the time. She has skin in the game. She came through HISD.— Fred Woods, Houston parent, third-generation HISD family

That is what accountability actually is. Not a complaints process. A person with a stake in the community, in the schools, in you and your children.

And now the part that isn't hypothetical

In 2023, Texas removed Houston's elected school board.

The Texas Education Agency dissolved the board Houston voted for and installed a Board of Managers it appointed instead. More than 180,000 students, most of them Black and Latino. The stated reason was the performance of a single high school — Wheatley, a historically Black school in a neighborhood the state had disinvested in for decades.

Parents did not vote on this. They were not asked.

Then the small things started going.

The librarian was fired first. The library became a storage room, then a room they send children to when they fail the daily quiz.

The paints went. “They say it was too messy, but I'm just sad because you could just wash it off.” — Gabriel, age 8

The bathrooms got locked for the first twenty minutes of every class, and the last twenty. Children had accidents.

Every lesson got a timer. Thirty seconds to discuss. “We just have to move on. There's not much a teacher can say other than we have to move on.”

The books are still in the library. The children can see them through the glass. They are not allowed to touch them.

We interviewed twenty-one people in Houston. Over half used the same word: prison.

After the state takeover school for my child became like prison. And those are her words.— Kourtney Revels, quoting her nine-year-old daughter
Being a student in HISD feels like being a prisoner.— Drapon Allen, 18
School became like a prison. I felt like they were watching our every move. My students would come warn me when the principal was coming.— MinhDan Tran, physics teacher

And the school board? Houston kept voting for one.

We did have an election this past November where we elected four school board positions, but the people we elected have no voting power.— MinhDan Tran
The room was filled with parents and students opposing the closures, pleading with them. And we were met with a unanimous vote to close all the schools.— Toshiba Dixon, parent, Port Houston Elementary

“The decision makers seem most accountable to the politicians in Austin that have assigned them to their positions. And in a democratic environment, that would be us. However, in this takeover environment, our voices have been displaced.”

— Toshiba Dixon, Houston

Your water
Your water

So you do the thing you're supposed to do.

Whose water is it?

The state didn't build it and doesn't pay for it.
Not here. This one is municipal.
Yours. And that turns out to matter enormously.

You call the city. You go to the council meeting — the people you elected, in the city you live in, that owns the pipes.

And here's the thing about a municipal water system: your city built it. Your neighbors paid for it, through decades of taxes and rates. It is, in the most literal sense, yours.

A city builds and pays for its own water system. Who should get to run it?

The state didn't build it and doesn't pay for it.
Hold onto that answer. It is the one that unfortunately won.
That is the answer almost everyone gives. It is not what happened.

Jackson, Mississippi went further than most cities ever do.

Eighty percent Black. And alongside the ordinary machinery of a city, Jackson built something almost no American city has: a People's Assembly — an actual standing structure where residents make decisions together and the city governs alongside them rather than at them.

And now the part that isn't hypothetical

Then the water failed — and the state arrived to take it, not to fix it.

After the 2022 crisis left the city without safe drinking water, the state moved to regionalize the system, shifting control outward toward surrounding counties — smaller, whiter, and not the people who built or use it.

One state can change a law we agreed to as a community.— Rukia Lumumba, Jackson

And it did not stop at the water.

In the same period the legislature passed House Bill 1020. It mandated the appointment of special judges and prosecutors by state officials in majority-Black Hinds County, which includes the City of Jackson. It also let Capitol Police — a state force — take effective control of policing in part of the city.

Read plainly: State legislators with no connection to Jackson shifted authority over a county's criminal justice system away from democratically elected judges and prosecutors chosen by Black voters.

And it took the money with it. 18% of the tax revenue that should go to the City of Jackson now goes to the state instead — to fund the new judiciary it imposed.

First you can't drink it. Then you can't decide about it.

A city that governs by assembly, governed by appointment.

Jackson has one of the most developed models of shared community governance in the country. But thanks to this legal tool some refer to as “preemption,” it is being run, in significant part, by people its residents did not choose.

Both things are true at once. And the second one is not an accident of history — it is the response to the first.

Widen out
All three paths arrive here

The common impact.

Houston's school board. Jackson's water and its courts. The removal of power from Virginia’s localities.

And St. Louis, where in 2025 the Missouri legislature seized control of the city's police department, handed it to a state-appointed board, and required the city to spend a quarter of its general revenue on a department it no longer controls.

The last time Missouri did that was 1861, on the eve of the Civil War.

Go back in time ↓

Journey back a hundred and fifty years and watch it all happen the first time.

What they built

After the Civil War, Black communities across the South did exactly what you just did. They organized. They built schools and hospitals. They elected local officials, passed local rules that made their lives better, cared for each other. In some places they governed outright.

What was taken

Within a decade, white paramilitary coalitions calling themselves “Redeemers” took back the state legislatures — and used them to abolish local governments altogether. They rewrote state constitutions to eliminate Black-led governance.

They did not have to beat those communities at the local level. They moved the decision somewhere those communities could not reach.

It was the same plot then as the one today.
