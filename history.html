150 years

The methods have changed.
The purpose has not.

Every era on this timeline begins the same way: a community builds something. Then the decision gets ripped from their reach. Read it end to end. There is a direct line from slavery through to today’s growing efforts to steal decisionmaking power from Black and brown communities.

1Pre-Civil WarPre-1865

Before there was a fight over local power, there was a system built to make sure Black people had none at all. Every mechanism that follows — the override, the takeover, the appointed board — is a descendant of this arrangement.

Pre-1865

The original power grab

Before 1865, Black people in the United States had no legal personhood. Slavery meant total control over Black life, enforced by law and upheld by violence. Black people were denied all rights to self-governance, land ownership, and power. Plantation owners in the South exercised total legal and economic control. State and local governments were organized entirely around the preservation of white supremacy.

2The Reconstruction Era1865–1877

For a brief window, Black communities across the South built and ran things: schools, hospitals, town governments, local law. This era is the high-water mark of what was possible — and the beginning of the legal architecture built to end it.

April 9, 1865

The End of the Civil War

Communities of newly freed and formerly enslaved people began building power for themselves. They built schools and hospitals. They gained political, social, and economic power. They were elected to local and state offices, passed legislation, voted, and organized for one another.

April 14, 1865

The Assassination of Abraham Lincoln · Presidency of Andrew Johnson

Reconstruction formally began when Andrew Johnson was sworn in as the 17th President. Johnson outlined how new state governments would be created. States could manage their own affairs — and some former Confederate states used that latitude to enact the Black codes, laws intended to maintain white supremacy as the law of the land. The codes required Black people to sign yearly labor contracts, guaranteeing white employers cheap and steady labor, and reestablished plantation discipline and control.

1868

“Dillon’s Rule” Introduced

Introduced in a decision by Iowa Supreme Court Justice John F. Dillon, Dillon’s Rule holds that localities have only the governing powers a state has explicitly given them by law. Otherwise they have no decisionmaking power whatsoever.

→ this is Virginia’s story
Proposed addition · April 13, 1873

The Colfax Massacre

During disputes over the gubernatorial and local parish elections, Black men occupied and set out to defend the courthouse in Colfax, Louisiana, in an attempt to prevent a white supremacist takeover of their local governance. In response, a large white mob — including members of the Ku Klux Klan and former Confederate soldiers — surrounded them, fired a cannon, and set fire to the courthouse. Around 150 Black men were murdered defending local power. The Colfax massacre was the deadliest single incident of racial violence of the Reconstruction era.

Proposed addition · 1876

United States v. Cruikshank

After the Colfax massacre, the United States Supreme Court ruled that the federal government could not prosecute private individuals for violating Black citizens’ rights. According to the Court, the power to protect Black citizens against white supremacist violence and stolen power was held solely by state governments — which in many places were led near exclusively by white supremacists. Federal protection withdrew, and the states that had just been recaptured were handed sole authority over the people they were disenfranchising.

3Post-Reconstruction / “Redemption”1875–1900

The backlash was organized, armed, and legislative. Having lost at the local level, white power moved the decision upward — to state legislatures and state constitutions, out of reach of the communities it was aimed at.

“It is one of the oldest tricks in their book.”— From The Roots, Advancement Project
1875

The First Home Rule State: Missouri

Home rule is the opposite of Dillon’s Rule. Home rule allows local governments within a state to largely govern themselves — but the state keeps the power to deny a locality’s authority on certain issues. Missouri’s new constitution was the first ever to break from Dillon’s Rule, allowing cities with more than 85,000 residents to frame and adopt their own local charters and laws. St. Louis became the first city to take it up.

1875

“Redeemers” Dismantle Local Governance

White paramilitary coalitions across the country calling themselves “Redeemers” took back control of state legislatures and used them to abolish township governments, violently destroying the multiracial democracy Black communities were beginning to build. They rewrote state constitutions to eliminate Black-led governance. Their strategy was disenfranchisement, retrenchment, and the weakening of Black power in order to rebuild their own.

Proposed addition · 1890

The Mississippi Constitution

Mississippi wrote a constitution designed to disenfranchise Black voters and strip them of any say in their daily lives without explicitly saying so — poll taxes, literacy tests, an understanding clause. Within twenty years, state after state copied it nearly word for word. One state writes a template; the rest adopt it.

Proposed addition · 1898 · highest priority

The Wilmington Coup

A duly elected, multiracial local government in Wilmington, North Carolina was overthrown by armed force. Officials were driven out of the city at gunpoint, Black residents were massacred, and the state's largest Black-majority city was permanently remade. It is the only successful coup d'état against a local government in American history.

4Jim Crow Era1900s–1940s

Jim Crow is usually told as a story of signs and segregated rooms. It was also a story about who was allowed to govern. The court decisions and state constitutions of this era made state override of local Black political power routine rather than exceptional.

Proposed addition · 1896

Plessy v. Ferguson

The United States Supreme Court ruled that “separate but equal” was constitutional — and in so doing not only approved separate bathrooms, separate schools and other overt practices of racial segregation and humiliation, but underscored the broad police power of state governments to control and regulate local health, safety and public order. Plessy established the legal architecture that made state override of local Black political power routine rather than exceptional.

Proposed addition · 1901

The Alabama Constitution

Written explicitly — by its framers' own stated admission — to establish white supremacy, and drafted to strip local governing authority so thoroughly that Alabama counties still cannot act without state legislative permission today.

→ a throughline to the legal regime in Virginia
5Civil Rights Movement1950s–1970s

As Black communities won at the ballot box and in the courts, states answered by taking the decisions away from the places where those wins were happening — schools, workplaces, city councils.

“The methods have changed but the purpose remains the same.”— From The Roots, Advancement Project
1944

Right to Work Laws

In Florida and Arkansas, a series of laws designed to strip workers of the power to organize made it harder for workers to form unions and negotiate for wages, benefits, and conditions. Rooted in racial capitalism, designed to keep Black and white workers from organizing together for power, and promoted by corporate-funded groups and segregationist politicians. These laws were an early example of how state laws were used to steal local Black and brown power.

Proposed addition · 1956–1959 · high priority

Massive Resistance

After Brown v. Board of Education overruled Plessy v. Ferguson and ruled that “separate,” segregated schools were in fact not “equal,” Virginia’s legislature stripped local school boards of the authority to desegregate and seized control of pupil assignment statewide. When some localities moved to integrate anyway, the state closed their public schools entirely rather than let a local decision stand.

→ The continuing fight for home rule in Virginia → The active state takeover of schools in Houston
1953

Landmark Reforms Surrounding Home Rule

The American Municipal Association — later the National League of Cities — published the most recent proposed reform of home rule, prompting states to update their constitutions and broaden local initiative power. Modern Constitutional Provisions for Municipal Home Rule reversed the traditional Dillon's Rule assumption, holding that home rule should be a default rather than something states granted. But it also held that states should keep broad authority to structure or override local law.

1975–1980

Mobile v. Bolden Upholds Voting Maps that Dilute Black Votes

In 1975, the Non-Partisan Voters’ League sued, alleging that Mobile, Alabama’s at-large form of government diluted the local Black vote in violation of the 15th Amendment’s extension of voting rights to freed slaves and the Voting Rights Act. An at-large government elects council members by the entire city population rather than by district. The United States Supreme Court, however, rejected the lawsuit and held — against all evidence to the contrary — that at-large elections are racially neutral.

6The Rise of Capitalism and Corporations1980s–1990s

Corporations discovered what segregationists already knew: if you cannot win in a city, you can go over its head. Industry money turned the override from a political weapon into a business strategy.

1980s

Smoke Bans

When local governments began adopting smoke-free public areas for public health, the tobacco industry responded by promoting and using the same laws that enabled stolen power to protect its own bottom line. Many of the anti-smoking regulations they targeted were in majority-Black cities.

Proposed addition · 1989 · high priority

New Jersey takes over Jersey City schools

The first state takeover of a local school district in American history. Within a decade the mechanism had spread to Cleveland, Baltimore, Chicago, Detroit, and dozens more — overwhelmingly districts serving Black and brown students.

7ALEC and Nationalized Preemption2000s–2010s

The American Legislative Exchange Council — ALEC — is a powerful lobbying group responsible for a significant number of preemption bills across the country. It drafts what it calls “model policies” and sends them to multiple states. You can usually tell ALEC is behind a bill when numerous states introduce legislation on the same topic in almost identical language. ALEC rose to power in the 1980s and 1990s, and became increasingly involved in blocking growth and reform in labor, housing, gun control and climate movements in the 21st century.

“None of this is random.”— From The Roots, Advancement Project
2000s

The Fight for $15 Movement

Local movements to raise the minimum wage began appearing across the country. ALEC responded by offering strategic planning to state lawmakers who opposed higher wages on how to use state law to override local decisionmaking. Since the movement was named, 25 states have passed legislation overriding higher local minimum wages.

2011

Florida Passes their Landmark Punitive Firearms Law

Florida passed a bill threatening lawsuits against city officials who enact gun safety rules. Now located in section 790.33 of the Florida Statutes, the law severely restricts local authority over firearms. Any existing or future local action is declared null and void, and local officials who attempt to legislate can be held personally liable and removed from office.

Proposed addition · 2011–2014 · high priority

Michigan's emergency managers · Flint

Michigan gave the state power to install appointed emergency managers who could override or replace elected local government outright. At the peak of this effort, a majority of Michigan’s Black residents lived under a government they had not elected — while most white residents remained in control of their lives and decisions. In Flint, an appointed manager’s decision poisoned a city’s water supply. Voters repealed the law by referendum in 2012; the legislature passed a near-identical replacement weeks later.

→ Explore the current state takeover of majority-Black Jackson, Mississippi’s water, courts and police
2016

Arizona Passes SB 1487

Arizona passed a law allowing the state to withhold funding from “noncompliant” cities: localities that pass legislation or take official action the state considers in violation of state law or the constitution (read: disapproves of).

8Abusive Preemption on the Rise2020–2024

The uprisings of 2020 were met with legislation. This is the period when the volume becomes impossible to explain as coincidence: hundreds of bills a session, filed across dozens of states, on the same handful of subjects.

Summer–Fall 2020

Legislative Backlash Follows Uprisings After George Floyd's Death

Millions saw the video of the murder of George Floyd, a Black man in Minneapolis, at the hands of police. They took to the streets against racism and police violence. While the overwhelming majority of protests were peaceful, states responded by passing laws blocking police reform at the local level.

Late 2020

COVID-19 Denialism Risks Public Health Protections Nationwide

The pandemic produced temporary protections for millions, including a federal eviction moratorium that kept families housed. As patience wore thin, the moratorium and other public health rules — including local mask mandates — were challenged in courts and legislatures. Mask bans have often been used as anti-protest legislation, and some states passed bans with no public health exemption at all.

2022–2024

Legislative Sessions Feature a Severe Uptick in Preemption Bills

Preemption legislation exploded across the country: 42 states introduced more than 650 bills. They banned water breaks for outdoor workers in Florida, stripped local control over housing and labor in Texas (HB 2127), and banned teaching students about race and gender in history. These bills increasingly carried criminal penalties, fines, and funding threats for anyone who legislated on issues their state had put off-limits.

9The Current Landscape2020s–Now

This is now. The takeovers on this list are running today, in real cities, against people who are still organizing against them.

“In a democratic environment, that would be us. However, in this takeover environment, our voices have been displaced.”— Toshiba Dixon, Houston parent
2020s–Now

Rises in Educational Preemption

At least 15 states have passed laws banning or restricting what public schools may teach about race, gender, or systemic oppression. These efforts whitewash the nation's history and silence local school boards run by, or serving, communities of color.

2023

Texas takes Houston · Mississippi takes Jackson's courts

The Texas Education Agency removed Houston’s elected school board and installed a Board of Managers it appointed, to steal power from local schools educating 180,000+ students, most of them Black and Latino. In the same period, Mississippi’s HB 1020 installed state-appointed judges and prosecutors in Hinds County (Jackson) and expanded a state-controlled police force.

→ Educational theft in Houston → State police takeover in Jackson, Mississippi
January 20, 2025

Endless Legislative Attacks on Communities Through Preemption

As of May 2025, the Local Solutions Support Center had tracked more than 685 introduced preemption bills, with 58 passed and 24 awaiting governors' signatures. The largest single category concerns immigration enforcement: many states have banned their cities from declaring themselves sanctuary jurisdictions or from refusing 287(g) agreements — the arrangements that pull local police and sheriffs into federal immigration enforcement. Other categories include bans on DEI programs in higher education, public health, “Death Star” bills that eliminate local authority across a wide range of issues at once, and elections.

March 26, 2025

Missouri State Legislature Passes HB 495

HB 495 seized control of the St. Louis Metropolitan Police Department and handed it to a state-appointed board, meaning the state now runs a city's police department. The state also controls police funding and limits the oversight the community fought for after the killing of 18-year-old Michael Brown in 2014. St. Louis is a predominantly Black city whose majority-white state legislature now controls its police department.

Preemption is being used today just as it was post-Reconstruction — for silencing Black and brown communities and stopping us from fighting for justice, power, and local autonomy.

— From The Roots, Advancement Project

One more thing you should be able to see from here.

The pattern is technically partisan, yes — Republican legislatures overriding Democratic cities. That framing isn't wrong, and it isn't enough. It flattens what is actually happening.

The cities on the receiving end are disproportionately Black, brown, and immigrant. The legal mechanism has been aimed at the same communities for a hundred and fifty years — through Reconstruction, Redemption, Jim Crow, right-to-work, Massive Resistance, emergency managers, and into this legislative session.

It is a racial-control story wearing a partisan suit.

They don’t attack what doesn’t matter.
